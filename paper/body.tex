\abstract
% context
{Astronomical archives answer positional queries from relational databases, and in the
PostgreSQL world two extensions dominate: Q3C, which keys each source by an integer
drawn from a quad-tree on a cube and stores it in a B-tree, and pgSphere, which stores
spherical geometry in a GiST tree. The first is compact and fast for points; the second
is general and indexes regions as well.}
% aims
{We ask whether the two can be combined: a single index that keeps the integer key and
the B-tree, yet answers cones, polygons, cross-matches and stored footprints in
archives' own geometry language, and whether it can beat each established extension on
its own ground.}
% methods
{We present \texttt{skycell}, a PostgreSQL extension that keys positions by their
order-29 HEALPix cell and turns a query region into index ranges. How finely to cut is
decided by a cost model -- the price of an extra index range against the rows it removes
-- with local source density read from the histogram \texttt{ANALYZE} already keeps.
Cells are classified conservatively against the region, and a planner support function
rewrites the predicate into B-tree range conditions plus an exact test, or, for a
constant cone, a custom scan walks the covering's ranges itself; a companion GiST
opclass family serves stored regions directly. Coverings are validated by brute force
against millions of catalogue points, including adversarial cones and polygons.
Performance is measured against Q3C 2.0.5 and pgSphere 1.5.2 on 10-- and 50-million-row
corpora -- synthetic, resampled from the real Gaia DR3 density field, and real DR3
positions -- in trials
where all three methods answer the same query back to back in randomized order, analysed
paired, warm and cold.}
% results
{Warm, skycell's custom scan beats pgSphere at every radius from 1\arcsec\ to 3\degr\ on
all three corpora: by 17--33\% on the synthetic two and 4--31\% on real Gaia DR3
positions, where the range rewrite alone loses below 3\degr; cold it is faster or level
at every radius. Its index is 214\,MB against pgSphere's 685\,MB -- 38\,GiB against
160\,GiB at Gaia DR3 scale for the pgSphere-plus-Q3C pair covering the same ground --
builds 30 times faster, answers convex polygons 2.7--4.4 times faster, cross-matches
20--33\% faster than \texttt{q3c\_join} (faster in all 24 cells of a sweep on real
positions) and 2.9--3.8 times faster than pgSphere, and its row estimates are wrong by a
factor 1.2 rather than 2.5--3.0. Its one free cost parameter is derived from the
relation's own statistics, its chosen cell order costs about 3\% against the best
available one, and its density estimate is good to within a factor 1.6 on ordinary sky
(low by 10--100$\times$ in a globular cluster core, which costs the covering chosen at
most a factor 1.8). At 50 million rows it stays 14--31\% faster warm at every radius
and its cold advantage widens, to a factor 2.7 at 1\degr. A \texttt{float4} GiST box key
matched to pgSphere's own representation beats every region-opclass alternative tried,
including pgSphere's native GiST, on every corpus measured. An attempt to sharpen the
covering's own search further looked like a clean win in execution time alone but
reversed under honest total-time accounting -- reported as a methodological caution,
not shipped as a default.}
% conclusions
{It can. A cost-based covering over an equal-area integer key, answered by its own scan
node, beats pgSphere at cone and polygon searches and Q3C at cross-matching -- each at
its own speciality -- from one index a third the size, while speaking ADQL's geometry
vocabulary and holding circles and polygons in one column. That makes it a strong
choice for an archive's positional layer; what remains is planning cost on small
relations.}

\section{Introduction}

Almost every question asked of an astronomical archive is positional: what is within
this cone, which observations cover this field, which of my sources match that
catalogue. In the Virtual Observatory these arrive as ADQL \citep{adql} through the
Table Access Protocol \citep{tap}, and the service must answer them with an index.

Two PostgreSQL extensions carry most of this traffic. Q3C \citep{q3c} projects the
sphere onto a cube, subdivides each face with a quad-tree, and numbers the cells along a
space-filling curve, so each source is one 64-bit integer in a B-tree. pgSphere
\citep{pgsphere} adds spherical types indexed with GiST over bounding volumes, so
regions can be stored and queried as data. The scale this has to work at is set by
archives already in operation: the ESA Gaia Archive \citep{gaiaarchive1, gaiaarchive2}
serves order $10^9$ sources and lists cross-match among its server-side capabilities
(Sect.~\ref{sec:xmatch}). Neither idea is new to astronomy -- the Hierarchical
Triangular Mesh \citep{htm} already keyed positions by a subdivided polyhedron, and the
zones algorithm \citep{zones} pushed a purely relational arrangement for cross-matching
-- but catalogue size has grown and most queries now arrive through a generated layer
\citep[e.g.][]{dachs} rather than being hand-written.

The two designs trade off: a linear key in a B-tree is compact and cheap to probe, but a
region must become a set of key ranges first, and that approximation's quality depends
on how finely the region is cut; a tree of bounding volumes needs no such step but has
larger, overlapping keys and imposes no order on the table. This paper presents
\texttt{skycell}, which keeps the integer key and asks a cost question at the point
where the classical approach fixes a constant: how finely should a region be cut? The
answer depends on two quantities the database already knows -- local source density and
the price of an extra index range -- and is computed rather than configured.

The measurements are built to survive scrutiny rather than flatter the method: every
query is answered by all three methods back to back in randomized order, analysed
paired; everything is measured warm and cold; and one corpus is density-matched to the
real Gaia DR3 sky while another is real, unresampled Gaia DR3 positions, not a density
field standing in for them. Sect.~\ref{sec:method} develops the B-tree index,
Sect.~\ref{sec:region-index} the GiST alternative for stored regions,
Sect.~\ref{sec:validation} what is proven versus tested, Sect.~\ref{sec:adql} the ADQL
surface, Sect.~\ref{sec:tests} the benchmark and results (source-like catalogues,
cross-matches, stored regions), and Sect.~\ref{sec:conclusions} what this establishes
and what remains open.

\section{The B-tree index}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{An equal-area integer key}
\label{sec:key}

A position is keyed by its order-29 HEALPix cell in the nested scheme \citep{healpix}
($12\cdot4^{29}\approx3.5\times10^{18}$ cells of about 0.4\,mas), computed by the
standard \texttt{ang2pix\_nest} arithmetic and stored as a signed 64-bit integer -- a
fixed-order index, not a multi-order key, so a B-tree orders keys along the HEALPix
curve. Three properties follow, only the first shared with Q3C's cube quad-tree. Cells
are \emph{nested}: the order-29 descendants of a cell $p$ at order $k$ are exactly the
contiguous interval
\begin{equation}
  \bigl[\, p\cdot 4^{\,29-k},\; (p+1)\cdot 4^{\,29-k} - 1 \,\bigr],
  \label{eq:interval}
\end{equation}
so a region covered by cells of any orders becomes a union of integer intervals a
B-tree answers with range scans (the MOC standard's \texttt{NUNIQ} encoding
\citep{moc}, used for stored regions, Sect.~\ref{sec:moc}, packs the order into the key
as $4\cdot4^{k}+p$, related by Eq.~(\ref{eq:interval})). Cells are \emph{equal area}, so
keys-per-interval is proportional to source density per solid angle: the
\texttt{ANALYZE} histogram is therefore a crowding map (Sect.~\ref{sec:density}) with no
extra machinery. And cells are \emph{standard} -- the same numbering underlies Gaia's
\texttt{source\_id} \citep{gaia} and IVOA MOC maps \citep{moc} -- so keys, coverings and
footprints are interoperable rather than private to one extension.

\subsection{Coverings decided by cost}
\label{sec:cost}

A query region is covered by cells whose union contains it; the covering becomes range
conditions on the key, and rows inside the covering but outside the region are removed
by an exact test. Cutting finer means fewer false positives and more ranges. With cells
of size $s$ covering a region of radius $r$ and local source density $\rho$,
\begin{equation}
  C(s) = n_{\rm ranges}(s)\,c_{\rm range} + \rho\,\bigl(A_{\rm cover}(s) - A_{\rm region}\bigr),
  \label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
both terms in rows: the second is the expected false positives, and $c_{\rm range}$
converts one additional index range into the false-positive rows it is worth avoiding --
a property of the installation (Sect.~\ref{sec:calib} measures it). A covering follows
the region's boundary, so for $s\ll r$ the ranges grow as $r/s$ while the wasted area
grows as the perimeter times $s$; writing $A_{\rm cover}\simeq\pi(r+\beta s)^2$ and
$n_{\rm ranges}\simeq\gamma r/s$ and minimising Eq.~(\ref{eq:cost}) gives
\begin{equation}
  s_\star = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma\,c_{\rm range}}{2\pi\beta\,\rho}},
  \label{eq:sstar}
\end{equation}
independent of region size: sky density and range price alone decide how finely to cut.
For $s\gtrsim r$ the balance differs, so rather than solve the branches the
implementation evaluates Eq.~(\ref{eq:cost}) at all 30 orders and takes the cheapest,
letting a range cap and a cell-size guard simply remove candidates. $\beta=0.7$ and
$\gamma=2$ are geometric; Sect.~\ref{sec:calib} shows the order choice is flat enough
near its optimum not to need them exact.

The same $0.25\degr$ cone covered in two places on the same catalogue illustrates both
regimes: at high Galactic latitude, 11 sources leave almost nothing to exclude, so the
model buys one range of large cells (5.6 times the cone's area); in the bulge, 1183
sources make the false positives cost more than extra ranges save, so the model cuts two
orders deeper (23 cells, 8 ranges, 1.3 times the area).

Two guards keep the model safe against its own inputs: a partly covered cell is never
allowed to exceed a multiple (64 by default) of the region's own area, bounding the
damage from a dense cluster the histogram underestimates; and gaps between ranges are
merged when the rows they contain cost less than the range they save.

\paragraph{The density estimate.}
\label{sec:density}
PostgreSQL's \texttt{ANALYZE} stores, for the indexed expression, an equi-depth
histogram: a sorted list of $B+1$ bounds $h_0 < h_1 < \dots < h_B$ in key space such
that each bucket $[h_{i}, h_{i+1})$ holds about $N/B$ of the table's $N$ rows. Because
the key is an equal-area cell index, the rows in a key interval $[a,b]$ are estimated
by linear interpolation within the buckets it spans,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{n}(a,b) = \frac{N}{B}\sum_{i} \frac{\bigl|[a,b] \cap [h_i, h_{i+1})\bigr|}{h_{i+1}-h_i},
  \label{eq:hist}
\end{equation}
and the corresponding density in rows per steradian is
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\rho}(a,b) = \frac{\hat{n}(a,b)}{(b-a+1)\,A_{29}} .
  \label{eq:rho}
\end{equation}
The estimate used by Eq.~(\ref{eq:sstar}) is $\hat{\rho}$ evaluated over the order-$m$
cell containing the region's centre, with $m$ chosen so that $s_m \approx 4r$: large
enough to contain enough rows to be a density, small enough to be local.

Three caveats: the quantity is a density, not the planner's own selectivity (computed
separately from the same histogram); Eq.~(\ref{eq:hist}) assumes rows are spread
uniformly inside a bucket, which fails for a cluster smaller than one, hence the area
guard of Sect.~\ref{sec:cost}; and the estimate is only as local as a bucket (with
$B=1000$, $N=10^7$, a bucket holds $10^4$ rows). Section~\ref{sec:estimator} measures how
close $\hat\rho$ lands to the truth.

\paragraph{Classifying cells: what is bounded and what is not.}
\label{sec:classify}
A cell must be classified against the region as inside, outside or straddling its
boundary, and the three outcomes are not symmetric: a cell called \emph{outside} is
dropped, so if that is ever wrong the query silently loses rows, while \emph{inside} and
\emph{straddling} cells both produce a superset the exact predicate (Sect.~\ref{sec:impl})
trims back. Only the outside decision therefore needs a bound rather than an estimate.

The bound used is
\begin{equation}
  R_{\rm cell}(k,p) = \min\bigl(\mathrm{maxpixrad}(k),\;
    \max_{i}\, \angle(\mathbf{c}_{k,p}, \mathbf{v}_i)\bigr),
  \label{eq:rcell}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{c}_{k,p}$ is the cell's centre, $\mathbf{v}_i$ its corners, and the first
term the analytic worst case the reference HEALPix implementation uses over the whole
sphere. A cell is outside when $\angle(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{c}_{k,p})-R_{\rm cell}>r+\epsilon$
for a cone of centre $\mathbf{q}$, radius $r$ (correspondingly per bounding half-space
for a polygon); $\epsilon=2\times10^{-13}$\,rad covers accumulated floating-point error,
five orders of magnitude below the leaf cell's resolution. The inside test is tighter,
using distances to the cell's own corners and edge midpoints directly. A tempting
outside-test alternative (distance to the chords between corners) is not a bound -- it
can under-estimate by a factor reaching 159 near order 18, invisible to every test we
had until it wasn't; Eq.~(\ref{eq:rcell}) avoids it, at a cost of 2.5\% more ranges and
no measurable time.

\subsection{Planner integration and ingestion}
\label{sec:impl}

The extension is written in C against the PostgreSQL 15+ server API: a geometry layer
with no database dependencies and a layer connecting it to the planner via a
\emph{planner support function} (\texttt{SupportRequestSimplify}, the mechanism PostGIS
uses for \texttt{ST\_DWithin}). Before path generation, a predicate such as
\texttt{point <@ circle(\ldots)} -- any region, unless it is a constant cone, which has a
scan of its own (below) -- is replaced by a disjunction of range conditions on the
key (\texttt{cell BETWEEN lo1 AND hi1 OR \ldots}) conjoined with an exact test, kept
only if the relation carries an index on the position. The index conditions are
ordinary range predicates, so the planner estimates their selectivity from the same
histogram, and the exact test's own argument carries the covering's occupied fraction,
keeping the product of the two estimates close to the truth. Two caches matter for
planning cost: the density model and index identity per backend, and coverings
memoised by region and statistics source (worth 45--70 and up to 50\,\textmu s per
plan on a TAP corpus). For joins, where the region is not constant, the rewrite emits a
fixed number of range slots per outer row, as \texttt{q3c\_join} does; the custom scan
below covers each outer row's cone instead, and Sect.~\ref{sec:xmatch} shows the
difference matters.

\paragraph{A custom scan for cones.}
\label{sec:customscan}
The rewrite leaves the ranges to PostgreSQL, which builds, estimates and costs an index
path for every arm of the disjunction: about 10\,\textmu s per range here, more than the
covering, and enough to decide a small cone warm (Sect.~\ref{sec:cones}). A cone is
therefore answered by a planner custom scan instead (\texttt{skycell.custom\_scan}, the
default; heap tables). The support function leaves the cone as one clause and supplies
its selectivity (the covering's expected rows times the cone's share of its area); a
path hook offers one custom path wherever a B-tree on the cell expression can serve
it, costed as PostgreSQL costs the same work. The node walks the covering in one of two
modes, chosen from the column's correlation as PostgreSQL chooses between an index and
a bitmap scan: one index scan per range in cell order when the heap follows the cell
order, or every range's TIDs into one bitmap, each heap page read once, when it does not;
rows are rechecked with the exact test. Since no range is planned, a range is priced as
the scan pays for it (Eq.~\ref{eq:crange-cs}). A cross-match cone, whose centre comes
from another relation's row, gets the node parameterized by that row on the inner side of
a nested loop, covering each row's cone with the ranges it needs; its row estimate is
measured at plan time on 32 sampled outer rows, since a density model cannot know that
cross-match targets sit on catalogue sources. Generic plans, correlated sub-selects and
the Q3C-shaped spellings (\texttt{skycell\_join}, \texttt{skycell\_radial\_query}) take
the same paths; a cone under an \texttt{OR}, and every non-cone region, keep the rewrite.
A regression suite checks the same rows with the scan on and off, in both modes, on
PostgreSQL 16 and 18.

\paragraph{Ingestion and maintenance.} The index is an ordinary B-tree on an expression:
one 8-byte key per insert, no method-specific rebalancing. Because the covering depends
on the histogram, statistics matter more than for a GiST index -- the area guard of
Sect.~\ref{sec:cost} bounds the damage from a stale one, and \texttt{ANALYZE} on the
10-million-row corpora here takes 0.5--1.4\,s.

\section{The region index}
\label{sec:region-index}

\paragraph{Regions as stored objects.}
\label{sec:moc}
A footprint is covered by a small number of cells written in the \texttt{NUNIQ} encoding
of the MOC standard and stored one per row in an ordinary B-tree. The footprints
containing a given position are those whose cell is one of that position's ancestors, a
short list of equality lookups; refinement stops when cells become negligible next to the
region, keeping the ancestor list short.

\texttt{skyregion} is one type internally tagged by kind rather than a family of
per-shape types, so a single footprint column holds circles and polygons together -- an
ObsCore \texttt{s\_region} column, say, mixes single-pointing circles with non-trivial
polygons in the same rows, covered by one function regardless of kind. pgSphere has no
equivalent: \texttt{scircle} and \texttt{spoly} are separate types, so a mixed-shape
column needs two columns in its place.

\subsection{A GiST opclass family for stored regions}
\label{sec:gist-family}

A \texttt{skyregion} column can carry its own GiST index instead of the B-tree rewrite
above, serving \texttt{\&\&} and both containment operators directly, keyed either by a
small number of spherical caps (exact for a circle at any radius, but larger) or a
plain axis-aligned 3D box. Shrinking the box key from \texttt{float8} (48 bytes) to
outward-rounded \texttt{float4} (24 bytes, pgSphere's own GiST representation) is sound
because recheck is already \texttt{true} regardless of key precision: a looser box
costs false positives the exact test removes, never rows. It is now the default region
opclass; the multi-cap key remains better for a column mixing very different region
scales, where box's looseness around a large circle costs more than its smaller key
saves. Measured against pgSphere's native GiST on isolated single-scale bands, a mixed
circle/polygon corpus, and the real \texttt{fpr} catalogue across all four region
strategies: Sect.~\ref{sec:tests-regions} (Table~\ref{tab:gistregion}).

The same shrink does \emph{not} transfer to a GiST key over \emph{points}
(\texttt{skypos\_cap4\_gist\_ops}, shrinking only the internal-node cap, since a point's
leaf stores its position exactly and leaf exactness is load-bearing there): leaf
entries dominate a GiST tree's page count, and the leaf layer here has nothing to
shrink, so no win results -- the structural reason the region shrink is safe (recheck
already \texttt{true}) does not hold for points. Numbers in Sect.~\ref{sec:tests-regions}.

\section{What is proven, and what is tested}
\label{sec:validation}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Proposition.} Let $R$ be a cone or convex polygon and let $\mathcal{C}$ be the
covering computed for it. If (i) every point of a HEALPix cell at order $k$ lies within
$\mathrm{maxpixrad}(k)$ of that cell's centre, and (ii) every angle computed by the
classification carries an absolute error below $\epsilon$, then every order-29 cell that
intersects $R$ lies in one of the ranges of $\mathcal{C}$.
\end{quote}

\emph{Proof sketch.} The descent starts from cells whose union is the whole sphere and,
at each step, keeps a cell, drops it, or replaces it by its four children (whose union is
the parent), so the kept union shrinks only where a cell is dropped, which happens only
when classified outside. With $R_{\rm cell}=\mathrm{maxpixrad}(k)$, (i) bounds every
point of cell $(k,p)$ within $R_{\rm cell}$ of its centre, so (ii) and the triangle
inequality on the sphere make $\angle(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{c}_{k,p})-R_{\rm cell}>r+\epsilon$
sufficient for the whole cell to miss the cone; the polygon case applies the same
argument per bounding half-space. Truncation (range cap, area guard, step budget) only
stops refinement, keeping a cell whole, so it cannot remove one either -- a cell
containing a point of $R$ intersects $R$ and is never dropped. $\square$

The proposition is conditional: (i) is a property of HEALPix, not of this work (the
reference $\mathrm{maxpixrad}$ is analytic, verified over $1.6\times10^6$ sampled
boundary points, largest observed ratio 0.9999; \texttt{skycell.exact\_cells = off}
rests on (i) alone, at the cost of Sect.~\ref{sec:classify}); (ii) is an assumption
about floating-point arithmetic, with $\epsilon$ chosen five orders of magnitude above
the plausible accumulated error and five below the leaf cell.

Because a covering that misses a cell silently loses rows, the implementation is also
checked by exhaustion: the HEALPix layer against known centres, round trips at all 30
orders, the nesting property, equal area ($\chi^2/\nu=1.14$ over 768 cells), and the
bounds of Sect.~\ref{sec:classify}, over $1.6\times10^6$ sampled points including poles
and zone boundaries; the covering against a 2-million-point clustered catalogue for
cones to $120\degr$ and convex polygons, 30\,000 adversarial cones (poles, zone
boundary, face edges, radii 0.02\,mas--10\degr) with 200 points each, and 7266
adversarial polygons with 866\,537 points -- all asserting zero false negatives, the
test that caught the error of Sect.~\ref{sec:classify}. Two SQL-level regression suites
require every indexed answer to equal a sequential scan with the exact predicate (240
cones, 60 polygons, both join forms, MOC lookup; and the ADQL surface, index-plan
presence, epoch propagation and frame conversions).

\section{The ADQL surface}
\label{sec:adql}

The extension exposes ADQL's own vocabulary: types \texttt{skypos} and
\texttt{skyregion} and functions \texttt{point}, \texttt{circle}, \texttt{box},
\texttt{polygon}, \texttt{contains}, \texttt{intersects}, \texttt{distance},
\texttt{area}, \texttt{coord1}, \texttt{coord2} and \texttt{centroid}, so a translator
emits the standard's spelling rather than a dialect, where pgSphere needs
\texttt{spoint}/\texttt{scircle}/\texttt{spoly} and Q3C a separate fast path per shape
with no region type at all; the operator form \texttt{pos <@ circle(\ldots)} is the one
a planner can answer from an index. Table~\ref{tab:ops} sets \texttt{skycell} against
the pgSphere-plus-Q3C pair for the constructs where they differ most.

\texttt{skyregion}'s text representation is IVOA STC-S \citep{stc} (\texttt{CIRCLE ICRS
$\alpha$ $\delta$ $r$} / \texttt{POLYGON ICRS $\ldots$}), the same syntax an ObsCore
\texttt{s\_region} column already carries, so such a column casts directly from, and
prints back as, the archive's own text with no translation step. For hand-written SQL
migrating from Q3C, \texttt{skycell\_radial\_query} and \texttt{skycell\_join} take
Q3C's own argument lists as a prefix replacement, both reducing to the canonical
\texttt{skycell\_cone}.

\texttt{skycell} also implements the IVOA UDF registry's epoch-propagation and frame
functions (\texttt{ivo\_epoch\_prop}, \texttt{ivo\_apply\_pm}, \texttt{ivo\_healpix\_*})
with the rigorous propagation of the Hipparcos catalogue \citep{hipparcos}, so a
proper-motion-aware cone search is an indexable cone widened by the largest motion of
interest, intersected with an exact test on the propagated position -- exercised by
round-trip tests (Barnard's star to sub-microarcsecond over 16 years) rather than
benchmarked.

\section{Tests}
\label{sec:tests}

\subsection{Three corpora}
\label{sec:corpora}

Three corpora, each answering an objection the previous one invites. The
\emph{designed} corpus is 10 million synthetic sources with a density contrast
resembling a real optical survey (35\% uniform, 45\% Galactic disk, 12\% bulge, 8\% in
200 clusters) -- chosen by the method's own authors. The \emph{Gaia} corpus answers
that without real positions: source counts from the ESA Gaia archive for every order-9
HEALPix cell ($3\,145\,728$ cells of 0.11\degr, summing to the published DR3 total)
give 10 million positions placed proportionally to each cell's count, the real sky's
crowding down to that map scale, Poisson below it by construction. The \emph{real}
corpus removes the remaining objection: ten million real Gaia DR3 positions, plus 1.6
million sources forming eight \emph{complete} fields at full density ($\omega$~Cen,
47~Tuc, M4, M13, Baade's Window, the Galactic centre, the LMC, and an off-cluster
control), since thinning alone dilutes crowding to 0.55\% of its true surface density --
used for the density-estimator and ablation measurements (Sect.~\ref{sec:ablation}),
where sub-cell structure is the whole question.

\subsection{Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

Q3C 2.0.5, pgSphere 1.5.2 and \texttt{skycell} each hold the same logical rows in their
own natural layout, all three physically ordered along a space-filling curve so heap
locality is held equal (Table~\ref{tab:repro}); row counts are compared on every query.
The measurement is organised as \emph{trials}: one query centre and radius, all three
methods answering it back to back in an order drawn at random (and recorded, so an
order effect can be measured rather than assumed away), repeated five times with the
query set traversed in random order each time. The analysis is paired -- median over
repetitions per method, ratio between methods for that query, so its own difficulty
cancels -- with a percentile bootstrap (4000 resamples) interval; a ratio is called a
difference only when its interval excludes 1. Everything is measured \emph{warm} (an
untimed pass precedes the measurement) and \emph{cold} (server restarted, caches empty,
first repetition only), with a separate randomized \texttt{EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS)}
pass recording planning time, execution time, buffer pages and row estimate apart from
each other. Cone radii span 1\arcsec\ to 3\degr\ with 16--400 queries each, half from
catalogue positions and half uniform on the sky; cross-matches join a 200\,000-row
probe table to the catalogue, half displaced by $\sim$0.3\arcsec; footprint queries ask
which of 20\,000 stored circles contain each probe.

Every column is \texttt{skycell}'s custom scan, its default
(Sect.~\ref{sec:customscan}). Warm, it is faster than pgSphere at every radius on all

synthetic ones and by 4--31\% on real positions (Sect.~\ref{sec:realcones}). The

\label{sec:phase}
This matters more than it sounds: for the range rewrite on the host of
Table~\ref{tab:repro}, measuring one method's whole grid then the next instead gives
ratios of 0.69--0.87 below 30\arcmin\ where the paired protocol finds 0.89--1.03, since the phase-ordered arrangement flatters whichever method runs later --
here, \texttt{skycell}. The paired number is the one we stand behind.

Cold, the custom scan is faster at every radius on both synthetic corpora except
1\arcmin, where the two are level (0.96--0.99): by 12--21\% at 1\arcsec, 10\arcsec\ and
6\arcmin, 25--31\% at 30\arcmin\ and a factor 1.6--2 at 1\degr\ and 3\degr, because it
touches a third of pgSphere's buffer pages at 1\degr\ (on average 59 against 155 on the
designed corpus, 85 against 282 on the Gaia one) -- a few microseconds of memory traffic when those pages are warm, the whole cost of the
query when they are not. Cold ratios depend on the storage under the page cache: a
first-touch read here costs 0.06--0.1\,ms, the container's own fast flash storage
(rewriting 18\,GB through direct I/O left it unchanged, so it is not a cache that could
have been dropped), so this is cold for PostgreSQL and the virtual machine on fast
flash. The first host's virtualised laptop disk took about 16\,ms per first-touch read,
so its cold results (Sect.~\ref{sec:scale-memory}) are a slow-disk regime, where every
page saved counts for more.

\paragraph{On real Gaia DR3 positions the result holds.} The resampled corpus places
positions uniformly within each 0.11\degr\ map cell; the real one keeps every scale of
clustering the map cannot see. On 10 million real DR3 positions (Table~\ref{tab:cones}'s
rightmost columns) the custom scan is faster than pgSphere warm at every radius, by 4\%
at 6\arcmin\ to 31\% at 3\degr. It plans in at most 0.004\,ms more than pgSphere and executes in
0.52--0.82 of its time, touching fewer pages at every radius (4.3 against 5.7 at
1\arcsec, 90 against 313 at 1\degr). The range rewrite, run in the same trials, is
1.27--1.56 of pgSphere from 1\arcsec\ to 30\arcmin\ and 1.25 at 1\degr\ -- the small-cone deficit an earlier revision of this
paper reported from the first host -- and the cause is its planning, 0.014--0.17\,ms over
pgSphere's against a small cone that pgSphere answers in about 0.05\,ms in total; the
custom scan is 0.57--0.70 of the rewrite's time there. Real sub-cell clustering does not
cost \texttt{skycell} extra rows fetched: it cost the rewrite one planned path per range,
which the custom scan does not spend.
Cold (Table~\ref{tab:cones}, last column), the two methods read separate copies of the
same rows, so neither warms the other's pages; every centre is new and kept apart from
every other, and the server is restarted before each method's pass at each radius. From
6\arcmin\ up \texttt{skycell} is faster by a factor 1.5--2.1 (0.48--0.67), reading
0.54--0.79 of pgSphere's pages; it is 0.87 at 1\arcsec\ and level at 10\arcsec\ and
1\arcmin, where a first-touch query reads two or three pages and one fewer page read is
worth about as much as the remaining difference.
\label{sec:realcones}

\label{sec:scope}

order effect the randomization controls is small but real, inflating variance rather

\paragraph{Does the advantage grow with the catalogue?}
\label{sec:scale}
Ten million rows is 0.55\% of Gaia DR3, so we repeated the protocol on a
50-million-row Gaia-density corpus, where pgSphere's 3439\,MB GiST index exceeds the
2\,GB \texttt{shared\_buffers} while \texttt{skycell}'s 1071\,MB B-tree still fits
(Table~\ref{tab:scale}). The advantage holds at every radius and grows where pages
decide. Warm, the custom scan is 14--31\% faster than pgSphere at every radius at
$5\times10^7$ rows (0.69--0.86): from 30\arcmin\ up the margin holds (0.69--0.75) as the
buffer gap widens (188 pages against pgSphere's 642 at 1\degr), while at an arcminute
and below it narrows a little (0.70--0.74 to 0.78--0.86), since both methods touch five
or six pages at any table size and \texttt{skycell}'s planning grows with density
(0.036 to 0.042\,ms at 1\arcsec). Cold, the gain grows with size from 30\arcmin\ up, to
a factor 1.9 at 30\arcmin, 2.7 at 1\degr\ and 2.4 at 3\degr, and holds below

$5\times10^7$ measurement in direction (0.94--1.09 below 30\arcmin, 0.68--0.80 above);
the custom scan's gain over it is at small radii, 0.81--0.85 of its time below
30\arcmin, and nil from 1\degr\ up, where both read the same pages. Size costs
\texttt{skycell} nothing at any radius and widens its lead for the queries whose pages

\paragraph{The cost model: its parameter, its input, and what it is worth.}
\label{sec:calib}

Equation~(\ref{eq:cost})'s one free parameter, $c_{\rm range}$, need not be a parameter
at all: it is a ratio of two costs PostgreSQL already models, one more index range
(a B-tree descent) against one false-positive row (its index entry, heap tuple, exact
predicate, and heap page amortised over the rows sharing it):
\begin{equation}
  c_{\rm range} = \frac{(\lceil\log_2 N\rceil + H + 1)\,50\,c_{\rm op}}
    {c_{\rm tuple} + c_{\rm itup} + k\,c_{\rm op} + c_{\rm page}/n_{\rm pp}},
  \label{eq:crange}
\end{equation}
with $n_{\rm pp}=\mathrm{reltuples}/\mathrm{relpages}$. Every input is already at hand, so
\texttt{skycell.range\_cost} defaults to evaluating it per relation (116 on the
catalogues; 93 on a wider-row ObsCore relation). Placed in the same trials as the fixed
constant it replaces, the two are indistinguishable, since the cost curve is flat
(sweeping four decades moves the median time under 25\% between 3 and 300): the
derivation buys not speed but that the parameter stops being a number someone has to
choose, and the order it picks is likewise close to optimal (about 3\% against one order
finer at 6\arcmin) for the same reason.

Equation~(\ref{eq:crange}) is the rewrite's price, and knowingly high: it charges the
descent's comparisons at 50 times what \texttt{btcostestimate} does and every page as a
random read, which stands in for the per-range planning the rewrite incurs and
Eq.~(\ref{eq:cost}) does not model. The custom scan plans no range, so it prices one as
it pays for it: the descent as \texttt{btcostestimate} charges it, plus two pages -- the
leaf the descent lands on and the heap page where the scan stops running sequentially
-- each priced at $p = f\,50\,c_{\rm op} + (1-f)\,c_{\rm page}$ by where it is expected
to come from,
\begin{equation}
  c^{\rm cs}_{\rm range} = \frac{\lceil\log_2 N\rceil\,c_{\rm op} + 50(H+1)\,c_{\rm op} + 2p}
    {c_{\rm tuple} + c_{\rm itup} + k\,c_{\rm op} + p/n_{\rm pp}},
  \label{eq:crange-cs}
\end{equation}
with $f=\min(1, \mathrm{shared\_buffers}/\mathrm{relpages})$ the fraction of the
relation the buffer pool can hold: a page in the pool costs what touching an index page
does, one outside it a random read. For the 10-million-row Gaia catalogue of the
custom-scan runs ($\texttt{random\_page\_cost}=1.1$, 120 rows per page) this is 34 rows
with the relation in the pool and 87 with none of it there, against 110 from
Eq.~(\ref{eq:crange}) for the same relation, so the custom scan cuts its coverings
finer.

\label{sec:estimator}
The model's input, $\hat\rho$ from Eq.~(\ref{eq:rho}), was called unbiased in an earlier
version of this work -- true of positions resampled from a density map with no structure
below $0.11\degr$, but not of real Gaia DR3 positions. Table~\ref{tab:estimator} compares
$\hat\rho$ with $\rho$ counted from the same relation: within 5\% at Baade's Window
and a factor 1.3 at the Galactic pole (where the sample holds a few dozen rows),
\emph{low by a factor 10--100} in a globular cluster core, \emph{high by a factor 3.7}
at the Galactic centre, as the histogram resolves a cluster while a query sits inside
it and averages it away once the query is larger (the failure Sect.~\ref{sec:density}
predicts, now measured on real positions). The estimate also moves between
\texttt{ANALYZE} runs, which sample the relation afresh: at 47~Tuc the median ranges
over 0.01--0.11 across ten. The consequence is smaller than the error: since
$s^*\propto\sqrt\rho$, an order of magnitude in $\rho$ is a factor three in cell size,
and the cost curve is flat across much of that -- the covering chosen in a cluster
core, now through the custom scan, is within 1.05--1.53 of the best fixed order, against
0.98--1.31 where the estimate is accurate (an earlier run found 0.96--1.55, and 1.76 in a
sample that put 47~Tuc a hundredfold low), and against 243--830 for a fixed order chosen
badly. The error costs something in cluster cores at
intermediate radii; it does not cost a wrong covering. The estimator should be judged
by the decision it produces, not the number it reports.

The comparison with pgSphere confounds three things, separated on the real Gaia corpus:
\emph{heap clustering} is a large absolute effect but almost no differential one (ratio
0.64 clustered against 0.60 unclustered at $1\degr$); \emph{adaptive resolution} is worth
a great deal (against every fixed order 4--13 the penalty for choosing badly is
243--830$\times$, while the cost model lands within 1.02--1.58 of the best without
knowing the answer); and \emph{the index representation} is isolated by comparing
against Q3C instead, both B-trees over an \texttt{int8} key differing only in covering
strategy (Sect.~\ref{sec:cones}, with the caveat of Sect.~\ref{sec:scope}).
\label{sec:ablation}

\label{sec:tap}


\paragraph{The service-level A/B, and where the crossover is.} A benchmark of index
operations is not a benchmark of an archive. We ran the same build inside
\texttt{egernia} \citep{egernia}, a TAP 1.1 server, against its published 500\,096-row
ObsCore corpus: one database holding both indexes, two otherwise identical service
processes differing only in how they translate \texttt{CONTAINS}, interleaved over six
query classes at 1, 8 and 32 concurrent clients. Both targets pass the conformance and
cross-server agreement gates on all eleven portable classes, identical rows through
both, and the performance result is a tie (throughput ratios 0.96--1.06) although at the
database level \texttt{skycell}, through the range rewrite of the first host, is
1.2--1.45 times \emph{slower} per query: a cone over
half a million rows costs a fraction of a millisecond either way against roughly 10\,ms
of CPU in the service's own Python, so the database cannot move throughput. That
explanation predicts a crossover at larger relations, checked by building the same
ObsCore-shaped relation (observations in groups of 128 sharing a field centre, clustered
by collection and field) at three sizes and asking each the same 30 queries
(Table~\ref{tab:crossover}). Field and query centres are a stored set drawn once from the
DR3 density map (\texttt{bench/data/}), and the relation at a given size is the same on
every run. Earlier revisions of this table took the field centres from the first rows of
whichever catalogue corpus was loaded; the resampled-Gaia corpus is generated in HEALPix
order, so those rows were one patch of sky and every observation was packed into it (a
2\degr\ cone returned $1.2\times10^5$ rows at $10^7$, against $8\times10^3$ now), and the
result moved with the corpus -- the irreproducibility those revisions reported, not a
property of either index. Like the synthetic columns of Table~\ref{tab:cones}, it was
measured with \texttt{skycell}'s custom scan (Sect.~\ref{sec:cones}), and the range
rewrite alongside, on PostgreSQL 18.6 in a 4-core, 15\,GB cloud container, not the host
of Table~\ref{tab:repro}. The predicted crossover is there. The
small cone (Q05) is 1.14 times slower than pgSphere at $5\times10^5$ rows and at parity
by $10^7$; the cone with catalogue cuts (Q07) is at parity up to $2\times10^6$ and 20\%
faster at $10^7$; the degree-scale cone (Q06) is never slower and 25\% faster at $10^7$,
where pgSphere reads 939 buffer pages to \texttt{skycell}'s 591; the empty cone (Q12),
a few hundredths of a millisecond either way, is faster at every size. Run with the range
rewrite instead (in the same trials, \texttt{bench/results-pg18/crossover-stored/}),
the same queries reach nearly the same ratio on Q06 at $10^7$ (0.78), but the small cone
stays 2.1--3.5 times slower and Q07 does not cross: for degree-scale cones the margin is
the index, for the small ones it is the custom scan, which at $10^7$ plans Q05 in
0.024\,ms against the rewrite's 0.086 and executes it in 0.091\,ms against 0.168: the
rewrite's plan is a \texttt{BitmapOr} with an index descent and a bitmap per range, where
the custom scan walks all its ranges into one bitmap. The three translations return identical rows
for every query, size and run.

\paragraph{When the index no longer fits in memory.} Tested directly in a 3\,GiB
container (768\,MB shared buffers) holding a 22\,GB, 20.5-million-row ObsCore-shaped
relation (GiST index $1.51\times$ the buffer pool, \texttt{skycell} $0.57\times$):
\texttt{skycell} answers 4.2--4.8 times faster over 3500 uniform 2\degr\ cones, serving
53\% of its index accesses from the pool against pgSphere's 9\%. The 22\,GB heap takes
most of the pool regardless, so neither index is fully resident -- this measures a
smaller index winning a contested pool -- and interleaving both translations in one
pass instead of separate phases (each then leaving the heap warm for the other) puts
the gap at $1.6\times$ rather than $4.2$.

\subsection{Crossmatches}
\label{sec:xmatch}
\label{sec:otherops}

Convex polygons of 0.05\degr--2\degr\ take a median 0.281\,ms on the Gaia corpus against
pgSphere's 0.767\,ms and Q3C's 1.864\,ms, similarly on the designed corpus: a factor
2.7--4.4 either way, with pgSphere's mean and 95th percentile far above its median where
a polygon's bounding volume is a poor fit.

Cross-matching a user's uploaded target list is the operation the Gaia archive lists
among its server-side capabilities \citep{gaiaarchive1} and Q3C's own speciality
(\texttt{q3c\_join}), so it deserves the cone searches' protocol, with each method
warmed on its own query immediately before timing to avoid a spurious I/O-ordering
effect at these sizes (14\,GB against 2\,GB of \texttt{shared\_buffers}). In
\texttt{skycell} it is the same join written either way, \texttt{JOIN cat c ON
skycell\_cone(c.cell, c.ra, c.dec, p.ra, p.dec, r)}, answered by the range rewrite's
fixed slots or by the custom scan's covering per probe (Sect.~\ref{sec:customscan}).
\texttt{q3c\_join} is the fastest established method, 2.4--2.6 times faster than
pgSphere. At $10^7$ rows (Table~\ref{tab:xmatch}), the custom scan makes the join
25--29\% faster than
\texttt{q3c\_join} and 3.2--3.7 times faster than pgSphere, returning the same rows as
\texttt{q3c\_join} in every trial; run through the rewrite in the same trials it is
again not distinguishable from \texttt{q3c\_join} (0.99--1.17). Written as
\texttt{skycell\_join}, the spelling a \texttt{q3c\_join} user would write, over an
expression index on \texttt{skycell\_ang2cell(ra, dec)}, it is the same node at the same
speed (0.96--0.97 of the \texttt{skycell\_cone} form, not distinguishable), 27--33\%
faster than \texttt{q3c\_join}. The difference is per-probe work, not the sky scanned: the rewrite's plan is
fixed at four range slots per probe, a \texttt{BitmapOr} whose every arm descends the
index whether the probe's covering filled it or not (12.6 buffer pages per probe in one
block at 1\arcsec), where the custom scan walks the probe's own covering, 1.2 ranges on
average, in one node (4.3 pages). The covering itself is no tighter -- the sky it scans,
as a multiple of the sky asked for, still peaks around 105 near 0.25--1\arcsec, since the
cost model won't cut finer where extra ranges cost more than the area saved, and falls to
1.07 above 30\degr.

\paragraph{Estimates, plans and joins.}
\label{sec:joins}
A spatial predicate is rarely alone: three shapes (a cone with a catalogue cut, joined to
an observation table, and a three-way join) through each method show skycell's estimate
advantage carrying over (median error 1.15--1.88 against 1.63--2.33 for Q3C and
2.50--3.00 for pgSphere), but the plans are the same -- all three chose the same strategy
in 134 of 135 queries. Better estimates are demonstrated, better plans are not: an
accurate estimate is insurance against a query we did not write, and we have not shown
that insurance paying off on one we did.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

\texttt{skycell} keeps Q3C's compact integer key and B-tree while answering the region
queries pgSphere was built for, and treats a covering's depth as a cost question --
local source density against the price of an index range -- rather than a constant.
Measured paired against both, it beats each on its own ground. Against pgSphere, at the
cone search, its custom scan is faster at every radius from 1\arcsec\ to 3\degr\ warm
(17--33\% on the synthetic corpora, 4--31\% on real Gaia DR3 positions) and faster or
level cold (up to 2$\times$), and it answers convex polygons 2.7--4.4$\times$ faster
(Sect.~\ref{sec:cones}). Against Q3C, at the cross-match Q3C is built for, it is
20--33\% faster than \texttt{q3c\_join}, on resampled and real positions alike, and
2.9--3.8$\times$ faster than pgSphere (Sect.~\ref{sec:xmatch}). It does so with an index a third the size of pgSphere's (38
against 160\,GiB at Gaia DR3 scale for the pgSphere-plus-Q3C pair covering the same
ground), built thirty times faster, with row estimates good to a factor 1.2 rather than
2.5--3.0, from a cost model whose one parameter is derived from the relation's own
statistics rather than configured. Scale does not erode the advantage: at 50 million rows the
custom scan stays 14--31\% faster warm at every radius, and its cold lead for regions of
30\arcmin\ and above widens, to a factor 2.7 at 1\degr\ (Sect.~\ref{sec:scale}). For stored regions, a GiST opclass family with a \texttt{float4}
box key beats every alternative tried, pgSphere's native GiST included, on every corpus
measured (Sect.~\ref{sec:tests-regions}).

The interface matters as much as the speed. \texttt{skycell} speaks ADQL's own
vocabulary with an operator form the planner answers from an index; its stored regions
read and write as IVOA STC-S text with no translation step; and because
\texttt{skyregion} is tagged by kind rather than split into a family of types, one
column holds circles and polygons together, which pgSphere cannot. Neither established
extension closes these gaps alone. One compact index serving points, regions and
cross-matches through the language archives already use makes \texttt{skycell} worth
considering as the default positional index of a PostgreSQL archive, and in particular
as a TAP service's geometry layer (Sect.~\ref{sec:tap}).

The limits are specific. Every custom-scan number comes from one type of 4-vCPU
cloud host (Table~\ref{tab:repro}), on fast flash storage (0.06--0.1\,ms per cold page
read), so the cold margins are those of fast storage; on a slow disk, where each page
saved costs more, they would likely be wider. On real positions the cold small cones are level rather than won
(Sect.~\ref{sec:realcones}). On a small relation the small cone still favours pgSphere
(1.14 at $5\times10^5$ rows, Table~\ref{tab:crossover}), and a TAP server dominated by
its own Python leaves the choice immaterial. Better row estimates did not, on the join
shapes we wrote, produce better plans.

What remains expensive is planning: a covering costs 0.015--0.12\,ms even cached, against
0.006--0.008\,ms for a GiST predicate needing no such step. Of three attempts to cut it,
one paid off modestly: filling the per-order chord bounds lazily changes no covering and
cuts planning by about 1\% (95\% bootstrap CI excluding 1). An SP-GiST class over the
cell hierarchy shrinks the duplicate-position tolerance by an order of magnitude, so it
stays exploratory. Letting the covering search try finer orders than
Eq.~(\ref{eq:sstar})'s closed form (\texttt{skycell.probe\_orders}, off by default) is
worth 30--48\% in \emph{execution} time at several radii but reverses in \emph{total}
time against centres that do not repeat, since the extra classifications are paid at
plan time on every query -- a caution about measuring a cost-based index's own overhead,
not only what it saves. The density synopsis is the other limit: an equi-depth histogram
cannot see a cluster smaller than one of its buckets, and raising the statistics target
does not help; the area guard bounds the damage, and a multi-order count map
(\texttt{typanalyze}) is implemented as an option awaiting a measurement that shows it
helps.

None of these limits touches the central result. For the queries archives serve most --
cones, regions and cross-matches on catalogues large enough for the scan to dominate --
a cost-based HEALPix B-tree is faster than the two extensions that carry that traffic
today, smaller, and closer to the language the queries arrive in.
