\abstract
% context
{Astronomical archives answer positional queries from relational databases, and in the
PostgreSQL world two extensions dominate: Q3C, which keys each source by an integer
drawn from a quad-tree on a cube and stores it in a B-tree, and pgSphere, which stores
spherical geometry in a GiST tree. The first is compact and fast for points; the second
is general and indexes regions as well.}
% aims
{We ask whether the two can be combined: a single index that keeps the integer key and
the B-tree, yet answers cones, polygons, cross-matches and stored footprints, and that
speaks the geometry language archives already use. We aim to establish where such an
index helps, where it does not, and what its cost model actually depends on.}
% methods
{We present \texttt{skycell}, a PostgreSQL extension that keys positions by their
order-29 HEALPix cell in the nested scheme and turns a query region into index ranges.
How finely to cut is decided by a cost model -- the price of an extra index range
against the rows it removes -- with the local source density read from the histogram
\texttt{ANALYZE} already keeps for the key column, which is a density map precisely
because HEALPix cells are equal area. Cells are classified conservatively against the
region, and a planner support function rewrites the predicate into B-tree range
conditions plus an exact test. Coverings are validated by brute force against millions
of catalogue points and against points drawn inside adversarial cones and polygons.
Performance is measured against Q3C 2.0.5 and pgSphere 1.5.2 on two 10-million-row
corpora -- one synthetic, one resampled from the real Gaia DR3 density field -- in
trials where all three methods answer the same query back to back in a randomized
order, repeated, analysed paired, warm and with caches dropped, and repeated again on a
50-million-row catalogue where pgSphere's index no longer fits in memory.}
% results
{Warm, skycell is faster than pgSphere by 6--11\% for cone radii of 1\arcsec--1\arcmin\
and by 31--37\% at 3\degr, and the two are indistinguishable between 6\arcmin\ and
1\degr; on the Gaia field skycell is 3\% slower at 6\arcmin. With caches dropped it is
faster at every radius on both corpora (ratios 0.39--0.95), touching a third of
pgSphere's buffer pages at 1\degr. It keeps an index of 214\,MB against pgSphere's
685\,MB -- 22.5 bytes per row against 72, which at Gaia DR3's $1.8\times10^9$ sources
is 38\,GiB against the 160\,GiB of the pgSphere-plus-Q3C pair an archive needs today to
cover both cone searches and cross-matching; on a machine that holds the first and not
the second, warm skycell runs 10--20 times faster than cold pgSphere -- builds it in 1.4\,s against 42\,s, answers convex polygons 2.7--4.4 times faster,
matches \texttt{q3c\_join} on cross-matching -- the operation Q3C is built for -- while
being 2.5--3.3 times faster than pgSphere at it, and its row estimates are wrong by a
factor 1.2 rather than 2.5--3.0. The cost model's one parameter is
derived from the relation's own statistics and the planner's cost factors rather than
configured, its chosen cell order costs about 3\% against the best
available one, and its density estimate is accurate to about 25\% on ordinary sky at
all. Raising the catalogue
to 50 million rows separates the two halves of the result: for regions of 30\arcmin\ and
larger the advantage grows (at 1\degr, 0.85 to 0.60 warm and 0.53 to 0.31 cold), while
for cones of an arcminute and below it does not move, because there both methods touch a
handful of pages at any table size and what is left is planning. An ObsCore-shaped relation measured at three sizes puts the
crossover with pgSphere between 0.5 and 2 million rows for degree-scale cones, and beyond
10 million for small cones on field-clustered data.}
% conclusions
{A cost-based covering over an equal-area integer key is a sound basis for a sky index,
and the right question is not whether it is faster but for which queries. Its cost is
paid per query and its benefit per row scanned, so it wins where enough rows are scanned
to amortise a covering -- degree-scale regions, polygons, cross-matching, cold caches --
and that boundary moves up in radius as the catalogue grows denser. On a Gaia-sized
catalogue a small cone search is not the workload this index improves.}

\section{Introduction}

Almost every question asked of an astronomical archive is positional: what is within
this cone, which observations cover this field, which of my sources match that
catalogue. In the Virtual Observatory these questions arrive as ADQL
\citep{adql} through the Table Access Protocol \citep{tap}, and the service has to turn
them into something a relational database can answer with an index.

Two PostgreSQL extensions carry most of this traffic. Q3C \citep{q3c} projects the
sphere onto a cube, subdivides each face with a quad-tree, and numbers the cells along
a space-filling curve; each source is one 64-bit integer in a B-tree, and the table can
be sorted along the same curve so that neighbours on the sky are neighbours on disk.
pgSphere \citep{pgsphere} takes the other route: it adds spherical types -- points,
circles, polygons, boxes -- and indexes them with GiST over bounding volumes, so that
regions can be stored and queried as data, not only as query arguments.

The scale this has to work at is set by archives already in operation. The ESA Gaia
Archive \citep{gaiaarchive1, gaiaarchive2} serves a five-parameter astrometric
catalogue of order $10^9$ sources through an extended TAP interface, and its published
description puts cross-match -- matching a list of positions a user has uploaded
against the catalogue -- among the server-side capabilities implemented beside the
query interface itself, under the principle of moving the code close to the data rather
than the data to the user. Cross-match is therefore not a synthetic benchmark
operation: it is a service an archive of this kind is expected to provide, and
Sect.~\ref{sec:xmatch} treats it as a first-class result rather than an afterthought.

Neither idea is new to astronomy. The Hierarchical Triangular Mesh \citep{htm} had
already keyed positions by a subdivided polyhedron, and the zones algorithm
\citep{zones} showed how far a purely relational arrangement -- declination strips and
a B-tree -- can be pushed for cross-matching. What has changed since is the size of the
catalogues and the fact that most queries now arrive through a generated layer
\citep[e.g.][]{dachs} rather than being written by hand, so the index has to serve a
translator's output rather than a tuned query.

The two designs trade off against each other. A linear key in a B-tree is compact,
cheap to probe and orders the heap, but a region has to be turned into a set of key
ranges before the index can be used, and a set of ranges is an approximation whose
quality depends on how finely the region is cut. A tree of bounding volumes has no such
approximation step and handles extended objects naturally, but its keys are larger, its
nodes overlap, and it imposes no useful order on the table.

This paper presents \texttt{skycell}, which keeps the integer key and asks a cost
question at the point where the classical approach fixes a constant: how finely should
a region be cut? The answer depends on two quantities the database already knows -- how
many sources sit in that part of the sky, and how much an extra index range costs --
and it can be computed rather than configured.

Because the interesting claims here are quantitative, the measurements are built to
survive scrutiny rather than to flatter the method: every query is answered by all
three methods back to back in a randomized order, repeated and analysed paired;
everything is measured both warm and with the page cache dropped; and everything is
repeated on a second corpus whose density structure comes from the real Gaia DR3 sky
rather than from the authors. Section~\ref{sec:method} develops the method,
Sect.~\ref{sec:impl} its integration with a query planner, Sect.~\ref{sec:validation}
what is proven and what is only tested, Sect.~\ref{sec:bench} the protocol,
Sect.~\ref{sec:results} the results, and Sect.~\ref{sec:tap} a TAP service and the
measured crossover. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} states plainly what these measurements
do not establish.

\subsection{An equal-area integer key}
\label{sec:key}

A position is keyed by the index of the HEALPix cell containing it at order 29 in the
nested scheme \citep{healpix}, computed by the standard \texttt{ang2pix\_nest}
arithmetic and stored as a signed 64-bit integer. At order 29 there are
$12 \cdot 4^{29} \approx 1.4\times10^{19}$ cells of about 0.4\,mas, so the key needs 63
bits and a PostgreSQL \texttt{bigint} holds it without reinterpretation.

The encoding is the plain nested index at a fixed order, not a multi-order key: the
order is implicit, so a key is directly comparable with another key and a B-tree orders
them along the HEALPix curve. Three properties follow, and only the first is shared
with the cube quad-tree of Q3C.

Cells are \emph{nested}: the order-29 descendants of a cell $p$ at order $k$ are exactly
the contiguous interval
\begin{equation}
  \bigl[\, p\cdot 4^{\,29-k},\; (p+1)\cdot 4^{\,29-k} - 1 \,\bigr],
  \label{eq:interval}
\end{equation}
so a region covered by cells of any orders becomes a union of integer intervals and a
B-tree can answer it with range scans. The \texttt{NUNIQ} encoding of the MOC standard
\citep{moc}, used here for stored regions (Sect.~\ref{sec:moc}), packs the order into
the key as $4\cdot4^{k} + p$; the two are related by Eq.~(\ref{eq:interval}), and
\texttt{skycell} converts between them rather than storing two representations.

Cells are \emph{equal area}. Every cell at a given order subtends the same solid angle,
so a count of keys in an interval divided by the interval's length in cells is
proportional to a source density per unit solid angle. The histogram that
\texttt{ANALYZE} keeps for the key column is therefore a map of how crowded each part
of the sky is (Sect.~\ref{sec:density}), without any extra statistics machinery.

Cells are \emph{standard}. The same numbering underlies Gaia's \texttt{source\_id}
\citep{gaia} and the IVOA's Multi-Order Coverage maps \citep{moc}, so keys, coverings
and stored footprints are interoperable rather than private to one extension.

\subsection{Coverings decided by cost}
\label{sec:cost}

A query region is covered by cells whose union contains it; the covering becomes range
conditions on the key, and rows inside the covering but outside the region are removed
by an exact test. Cutting finer means fewer such false positives and more ranges. With
cells of size $s$ covering a region of characteristic radius $r$ and a local source
density $\rho$,

\begin{equation}
  C(s) = n_{\rm ranges}(s)\,c_{\rm range} + \rho\,\bigl(A_{\rm cover}(s) - A_{\rm region}\bigr).
  \label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}

Both terms are in rows: the second is the expected number of false positives, and
$c_{\rm range}$ converts one additional index range into the number of false-positive
rows it is worth avoiding. It is therefore a property of the installation, not of the
sky: the ratio between the cost of opening one more B-tree range scan (a descent of the
tree plus a planner path with its own selectivity estimate) and the cost of fetching and
discarding one heap row. Section~\ref{sec:calib} measures it.

A covering follows the region's boundary, so for $s \ll r$ the number of ranges grows as
$r/s$ -- the curve enters and leaves the region once per boundary crossing, and the
number of such clusters is the classical measure of a space-filling curve's quality
\citep{hilbert} -- while the wasted area grows as the perimeter times $s$. Writing
$A_{\rm cover} \simeq \pi(r + \beta s)^2$ and $n_{\rm ranges} \simeq \gamma r/s$ and
minimising Eq.~(\ref{eq:cost}) gives

\begin{equation}
  s_\star = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma\,c_{\rm range}}{2\pi\beta\,\rho}},
  \label{eq:sstar}
\end{equation}

which does not depend on the size of the region: the density of the sky and the price
of a range decide how finely it is worth cutting. For $s \gtrsim r$ the covering is a
handful of cells and the balance differs, so rather than solve the branches the
implementation evaluates Eq.~(\ref{eq:cost}) at all 30 orders -- a few floating-point
operations each -- and takes the cheapest, which also lets a cap on the number of
ranges and a guard on cell size simply remove candidates. The constants
$\beta = 0.7$ and $\gamma = 2$ are geometric; Sect.~\ref{sec:calib} shows the choice of
order is flat enough near its optimum that they do not need to be exact.

Figure~\ref{fig:covering} shows the two extremes on the same cone radius: at high
Galactic latitude the covering is a single range of large cells covering 5.6 times the
cone's area, because there is almost nothing to exclude; in the bulge the same cone is
covered by 23 cells in 8 ranges covering 1.3 times its area, because there the rows
removed are worth the extra ranges.

Two guards keep the model safe against its own inputs. A histogram is a coarse synopsis,
and a dense cluster split by the space-filling curve into pieces smaller than one bucket
can be underestimated; a partly covered cell is therefore never allowed to exceed a
multiple (64 by default) of the region's own area, which bounds the damage to the
density-proportional behaviour a fixed-depth covering would have had. Gaps between
ranges are merged when the rows they contain cost less than the range they save.

\subsection{The density estimate}
\label{sec:density}

PostgreSQL's \texttt{ANALYZE} stores, for the indexed expression, an equi-depth
histogram: a sorted list of $B+1$ bounds $h_0 < h_1 < \dots < h_B$ in key space such
that each bucket $[h_{i}, h_{i+1})$ holds about $N/B$ of the table's $N$ rows. Because
the key is an equal-area cell index, the rows in a key interval $[a,b]$ are estimated
by linear interpolation within the buckets it spans,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{n}(a,b) = \frac{N}{B}\sum_{i} \frac{\bigl|[a,b] \cap [h_i, h_{i+1})\bigr|}{h_{i+1}-h_i},
  \label{eq:hist}
\end{equation}
and the corresponding density in rows per steradian is
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\rho}(a,b) = \frac{\hat{n}(a,b)}{(b-a+1)\,A_{29}} .
  \label{eq:rho}
\end{equation}
The estimate used by Eq.~(\ref{eq:sstar}) is $\hat{\rho}$ evaluated over the order-$m$
cell containing the region's centre, with $m$ chosen so that $s_m \approx 4r$: large
enough to contain enough rows to be a density, small enough to be local.

Three things are worth stating plainly. The quantity is a density per unit solid angle,
not a selectivity: the planner's own selectivity estimate for the rewritten range
conditions is computed separately by PostgreSQL from the same histogram. The
interpolation in Eq.~(\ref{eq:hist}) assumes rows are spread uniformly inside a bucket,
which is exactly what fails for a cluster smaller than a bucket -- hence the area guard
of Sect.~\ref{sec:cost}. And the estimate can only be as local as a bucket: with
$B = 1000$ and $N = 10^7$ a bucket holds $10^4$ rows, so below that scale
Eq.~(\ref{eq:rho}) returns the average over a region containing $10^4$ rows, which is
the correct answer to a different question. Section~\ref{sec:estimator} measures how
close $\hat\rho$ lands to the truth, and at which scales the truth itself is defined.

\subsection{Classifying cells: what is bounded and what is not}
\label{sec:classify}

A cell must be classified against the region as inside, outside or straddling its
boundary. The three outcomes are not symmetric. A cell called \emph{outside} is dropped
from the covering, so if that is ever wrong the query silently loses rows. A cell called
\emph{inside} is taken whole, and a cell called \emph{straddling} is refined or taken
whole; both produce a superset of the region, and the exact predicate that the rewrite
conjoins to the index conditions (Sect.~\ref{sec:impl}) removes whatever the covering
over-included. Only the outside decision therefore needs a bound rather than an
estimate.

The bound used is
\begin{equation}
  R_{\rm cell}(k,p) = \min\bigl(\mathrm{maxpixrad}(k),\;
    \max_{i}\, \angle(\mathbf{c}_{k,p}, \mathbf{v}_i)\bigr),
  \label{eq:rcell}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{c}_{k,p}$ is the cell's centre and $\mathbf{v}_i$ its four corners.
The first term is the analytic worst case over the sphere used by the reference HEALPix
implementation; the second is the cell's own extent, which is smaller for most cells.
A cell is outside when $\angle(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{c}_{k,p}) - R_{\rm cell} >
r + \epsilon$ for a cone of centre $\mathbf{q}$ and radius $r$, and correspondingly for
each of a polygon's bounding half-spaces. The margin
$\epsilon = 2\times10^{-13}$\,rad (40\,pas) covers the accumulated floating-point error
of the handful of dot products, cross products and inverse trigonometric calls
involved, each carrying a few units in the last place; it is five orders of magnitude
below the 0.4\,mas resolution of an order-29 cell, so it costs nothing.

The inside test may be, and is, tighter: it uses the distances from the query centre to
the cell's own corners and edge midpoints directly.

A tempting alternative -- deciding \emph{outside} from the distance to the chords
between corners, inflated by the edge's departure from its chord at the midpoint -- is
not a bound: where an edge crosses its chord plane near the midpoint that departure
collapses towards zero while the true one does not, by a factor reaching 64 at order 18.
The error it caused was $\sim$$10^{-8}$\,rad, below a leaf cell and invisible to every
test we had, which is why the covering uses Eq.~(\ref{eq:rcell}) instead, at a cost of
2.5\% more index ranges and no measurable time.

\subsection{Regions as stored objects}
\label{sec:moc}

A footprint is covered by a small number of cells written in the \texttt{NUNIQ}
encoding of the MOC standard and stored one per row in an ordinary B-tree. The
footprints containing a given position are those whose cell is one of that position's
ancestors, a short list of equality lookups; the exact geometry is then applied to the
candidates. Since a MOC's cells are disjoint by construction, no duplicate elimination
is needed. Refinement of these coverings stops when cells become negligible next to the
region, which keeps the ancestor list short.

\subsection{The query surface}

The extension exposes ADQL's own vocabulary: types \texttt{skypos} and
\texttt{skyregion} and functions \texttt{point}, \texttt{circle}, \texttt{box},
\texttt{polygon}, \texttt{contains}, \texttt{intersects}, \texttt{distance},
\texttt{area}, \texttt{coord1}, \texttt{coord2} and \texttt{centroid}, so that a
translator emits the standard's spelling rather than a dialect. The operator form
\texttt{pos <@ circle(\ldots)} is the one a planner can answer from an index; the
function form returns the same rows.

For hand-written SQL migrating from Q3C rather than from ADQL, the predicates
\texttt{skycell\_radial\_query(ra, dec, ra0, dec0, r)} and
\texttt{skycell\_join(ra1, dec1, ra2, dec2, r)} take Q3C's own argument lists: the
support function synthesises the index expression from the first two arguments, so
the change is a prefix replacement. Both reduce to the canonical
\texttt{skycell\_cone}, which serves the cone search and the cross-match with one
predicate because the only difference between them is whether the centre is a
constant.

Beside the geometry, archives need the astrometry that decides \emph{where} a source
is at the epoch being asked about. \texttt{skycell} implements the IVOA UDF registry's
names -- \texttt{ivo\_epoch\_prop}, \texttt{ivo\_epoch\_prop\_pos},
\texttt{ivo\_apply\_pm}, \texttt{ivo\_healpix\_index}, \texttt{ivo\_healpix\_center} --
with the rigorous epoch propagation of the Hipparcos catalogue \citep{hipparcos}, in
which the star follows a straight line in space so that parallax, proper motion and
radial velocity all evolve together. Frame conversions between ICRS, Galactic and
ecliptic coordinates are provided for the same reason.

An index built on catalogue positions cannot know where a star has moved to, so a
proper-motion-aware cone search is written as an indexable cone widened by the largest
motion of interest, intersected with an exact test on the propagated position.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:impl}

The extension is written in C against the PostgreSQL 15+ server API and consists of a
geometry layer with no database dependencies -- HEALPix arithmetic, region predicates
and the covering -- and a layer that connects it to the planner.

The connection is a \emph{planner support function}
(\texttt{SupportRequestSimplify}), the mechanism PostGIS uses for
\texttt{ST\_DWithin}. Before path generation, a predicate such as
\texttt{point('ICRS', ra, dec) <@ circle('ICRS', $\alpha$, $\delta$, $r$)} is replaced by
a disjunction of range conditions on the key expression, conjoined with the exact test:

\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
(cell BETWEEN lo1 AND hi1\\
 OR cell BETWEEN lo2 AND hi2 OR ...)\\
AND skycell\_in\_region(point(ra, dec),\\
\hspace*{6em}circle(...), 0.29)
\end{quote}

The key expression is not written by the user: the support function builds the
candidate expression from the predicate's position argument and keeps it only if the
relation actually carries an index on it, so the same query text works whether
positions are stored as two columns or as a \texttt{skypos} column. Because the index
conditions are then ordinary range predicates, the planner estimates their selectivity
from the same histogram, and the last argument of the exact test carries the fraction
of the covering that the region occupies, so that the product of the two estimates is
close to the truth.

Two caches matter for planning cost. The density model and the identity of the matching
index are cached per backend and dropped whenever statistics, the relation or the
relation cache change; coverings are memoised by region and statistics source, since a
service plans the same cone many times. Measured on a TAP corpus, the first cache is
worth 45--70\,\textmu s per plan and the second up to 50\,\textmu s for larger regions.

For joins, where the region is not a constant, the rewrite emits a fixed number of
range slots computed per outer row -- the structure \texttt{q3c\_join} uses -- while a
set-returning function \texttt{skycell\_cone\_ranges()} offers the alternative of a
variable number of ranges through a \texttt{LATERAL} join. The two differ in plan shape
rather than geometry, and Sect.~\ref{sec:otherops} shows the difference is large.

\paragraph{Ingestion and maintenance.}
The index is an ordinary B-tree on an expression, so its maintenance behaviour is
PostgreSQL's: an insert adds one 8-byte key, updates that do not move a source do not
touch it, and there is no rebalancing peculiar to the method. Two operational points
differ from a geometry index. Bulk ingestion benefits from loading in key order, since
that is also the heap order the queries exploit, and the cost of \emph{not} doing so is
the usual loss of locality rather than a degraded index. And the covering depends on
the histogram, so statistics matter more than for a GiST index: a relation whose
density structure has changed substantially since the last \texttt{ANALYZE} will be
covered for the sky it used to have. The guard of Sect.~\ref{sec:cost} bounds the
damage, and \texttt{ANALYZE} on the 10-million-row corpora here takes 0.5--1.4\,s.

\section{What is proven, and what is tested}
\label{sec:validation}

\subsection{A conservative-covering statement}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Proposition.} Let $R$ be a cone or convex polygon and let $\mathcal{C}$ be the
covering computed for it. If (i) every point of a HEALPix cell at order $k$ lies within
$\mathrm{maxpixrad}(k)$ of that cell's centre, and (ii) every angle computed by the
classification carries an absolute error below $\epsilon$, then every order-29 cell that
intersects $R$ lies in one of the ranges of $\mathcal{C}$.
\end{quote}

\emph{Proof sketch.} The covering is produced by a descent that starts from cells whose
union is the whole sphere and, at each step, either keeps a cell, drops it, or replaces
it by its four children, whose union is the parent. The union of the kept cells is
therefore reduced only where a cell is dropped, which happens only when it is classified
outside. Taking $R_{\rm cell} = \mathrm{maxpixrad}(k)$ in Eq.~(\ref{eq:rcell}), every
point $\mathbf{x}$ of cell $(k,p)$ satisfies
$\angle(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{c}_{k,p}) \le R_{\rm cell}$ by (i); hence if
$\angle(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{c}_{k,p}) - R_{\rm cell} > r + \epsilon$, then by the
triangle inequality on the sphere and (ii) every point of the cell is farther than $r$
from $\mathbf{q}$, so the cell does not intersect the cone. The polygon case is the same
argument applied to each bounding half-space. Truncation by the range cap, the area
guard or the step budget only stops refinement, which keeps a cell whole, so it cannot
remove a cell either. A cell containing a point of $R$ intersects $R$ and is therefore
never dropped. $\square$

The proposition is conditional, and both conditions deserve their status.
Condition (i) is a property of HEALPix, not of this work: the reference implementation's
$\mathrm{maxpixrad}$ is analytic, and we verify it numerically over $1.6\times10^6$
sampled boundary points at every order, the largest observed ratio of centre-to-point
distance to $\mathrm{maxpixrad}$ being 0.9999. The tighter corner term in
Eq.~(\ref{eq:rcell}) additionally assumes that the extremum of the distance from the
centre over a cell is attained at a corner, which the same scan supports (largest
observed ratio 0.96) but which we have not proven; a deployment that wants the
proposition to rest on (i) alone can set \texttt{skycell.exact\_cells} to off, at the
cost quantified in Sect.~\ref{sec:classify}. Condition (ii) is an assumption about
floating-point arithmetic rather than a theorem: $\epsilon$ is chosen five orders of
magnitude above the plausible accumulated error and five below the leaf cell.

\subsection{Brute force}

Because a covering that misses a cell silently loses rows, the implementation is
checked by exhaustion at three levels.

The HEALPix layer is checked against known cell centres, round trips between positions
and cells at all 30 orders, the nesting property of Eq.~(\ref{eq:interval}), equal area
($\chi^2/\nu = 1.14$ over 768 base cells), and the bounds of Sect.~\ref{sec:classify},
over $1.6\times10^6$ sampled points including the poles, the boundary between the polar
and equatorial zones, and the base-pixel edges.

The covering is checked against a 2-million-point clustered catalogue for cones from
1\arcsec\ to 120\degr\ and for convex polygons; against 30\,000 adversarial cones -- at
the poles, across the zone boundary, at $\alpha = 0$ and on face edges, with radii from
0.02\,mas to 10\degr\ -- with 200 points sampled inside each, half of them within a part
per billion of the rim; and against 7266 adversarial polygons at the same awkward
places, with 866\,537 points drawn inside them, again half of them a hair inside an edge
or a vertex. All assert zero false negatives, in both the seeded and the unseeded
covering path. This is the test that caught the error described in
Sect.~\ref{sec:classify}.

At the SQL level, two regression suites require that every indexed answer equals the
answer from a sequential scan with the exact predicate: 240 cones in two configurations,
60 polygons, both join forms and the MOC footprint lookup in the first; the ADQL
surface, the operator and function spellings over 120 random regions, the presence of
index plans (and their absence when the region is not constant), epoch-propagation round
trips and frame conversions in the second.

\section{Benchmark}
\label{sec:bench}

\subsection{Three corpora}
\label{sec:corpora}

The first corpus is 10 million synthetic sources with a density contrast resembling a
real optical survey: 35\% uniform on the sphere, 45\% in a Galactic disk (longitude
uniform, latitude exponential with a 3\degr\ scale), 12\% in a bulge (Gaussian,
7\degr), and 8\% in 200 clusters with dispersions from 0.02\degr\ to 0.5\degr.
Positions are drawn deterministically from a seed. We call it the \emph{designed}
corpus, because its density structure was chosen by the authors of the method under
test, which is exactly the objection such a corpus invites.

The second corpus removes that objection as far as we can. We queried the ESA Gaia
archive for the number of Gaia DR3 sources in every HEALPix cell at order 9
($3\,145\,728$ cells of 0.11\degr; the counts sum to $1\,811\,709\,771$, the published
DR3 total), and drew 10 million positions from that density field: each cell receives a
number of sources proportional to its Gaia count, placed uniformly inside it by
choosing a random order-29 descendant, which is exact because the descendants are equal
area. The result has the real sky's crowding -- the disk, the bulge, the Magellanic
Clouds, the scanning-law texture -- down to the 0.11\degr\ scale of the map, below which
it is locally Poisson. It is not a Gaia catalogue: no real positions are used, and
structure finer than a map cell is absent by construction. What it tests is whether the
cost model, which reads density at scales of a few times the query radius, behaves the
same on a density field the authors did not invent. We call it the \emph{Gaia} corpus.

The third corpus removes the remaining objection: it is made of real positions. We took
ten million Gaia DR3 sources with \texttt{random\_index} $< 10^{7}$. Because
\texttt{random\_index} is a precomputed random permutation of the catalogue, this is a
uniform random \emph{thinning} of the true point process: every position is a measured
position, and the two-point structure is preserved at every angular scale rather than
only above a map cell. What thinning removes is absolute crowding -- a cluster core is
still a cluster core, but at 0.55\% of its true surface density -- so we added $1.6$
million sources forming eight \emph{complete} fields at full density: $\omega$~Cen,
47~Tuc, M4, M13, Baade's Window, the Galactic centre, the LMC, and a control field off
47~Tuc. We call these the \emph{real} corpus, and use it for the density-estimator and
ablation measurements, where sub-cell structure is the whole question.

The real corpus is held twice: once in cell order, and once in \texttt{random\_index}
order, which is random on the sky. The second is an unclustered heap, and having both
makes physical layout a variable we can measure rather than a condition we impose
(Sect.~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\subsection{Targets and layout}

Q3C 2.0.5, pgSphere 1.5.2 and \texttt{skycell} each hold the same logical rows in their
own natural layout, all three physically ordered along a space-filling curve so that
heap locality is held equal (Table~\ref{tab:repro}). Queries are the documented
interface of each extension: \texttt{q3c\_radial\_query}, \texttt{spoint <@ scircle}
and \texttt{skycell\_cone}. Row counts are compared on every query.

\subsection{Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

The measurement is organised as \emph{trials}. A trial is one query centre and radius;
within a trial all three methods answer the same query back to back, in an order drawn
at random for that trial, and the position each method drew is recorded so that an order
effect can be measured rather than assumed away. Trials are repeated five times over the
whole query set, and the query set is traversed in a random order each repetition. The
analysis is paired: for each query we take the median over repetitions per method and
form the ratio between methods for that same query, so that the query's own difficulty
cancels; the reported interval is a percentile bootstrap (4000 resamples) over queries.
A ratio is called a difference only when its interval excludes 1.

Everything is measured in two cache regimes. \emph{Warm}: an untimed pass over the whole
query set precedes the measurement, so no method is measured on a colder cache than
another. \emph{Cold}: the server is restarted, so its 2\,GB of shared buffers is empty,
and the virtual machine's page cache is dropped, so the data must come from storage;
only the first repetition is used, since a second would no longer be cold.

Separately, a randomized pass under \texttt{EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS)} records planning
time, execution time, buffer pages and the planner's estimate against the actual count,
so that planning and execution can be reported apart from each other.

Cone radii span 1\arcsec\ to 3\degr\ with 16--400 queries each, half of the centres
drawn from catalogue positions (hence density-weighted) and half uniform on the sky.
Cross-matches join a 200\,000-row probe table -- half catalogue sources displaced by
$\sim$0.3\arcsec, half uniform -- to the catalogue. Footprint queries ask which of
20\,000 stored circles contain each of the 200\,000 probes.

\subsection{What this protocol still does not control}

The corpora are synthetic, one designed by the authors and the other derived from a real
density map but not from real positions. One machine, one storage device, one PostgreSQL
configuration. Absolute times are properties of that machine; only the comparisons are
meant to travel. The cold regime drops the page cache but cannot empty the device's own
cache. And the three extensions are compared as configured here: a different Q3C query
formulation or pgSphere index configuration could move their numbers, and
Sect.~\ref{sec:cones} reports one such effect that dominates Q3C's small-cone results.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

All three methods return identical row counts on every query of the grid. In the
cross-matches pgSphere returns one or two more pairs out of $\sim$100\,000, consistent
with its $10^{-9}$\,rad comparison tolerance counting sources exactly on the rim.

Warm, \texttt{skycell} is faster than pgSphere by 6--11\% for radii from 1\arcsec\ to
1\arcmin\ and by 31--37\% at 3\degr, on both corpora. Between 6\arcmin\ and 1\degr\ the
two are, on the designed corpus, indistinguishable; on the Gaia corpus skycell is 3\%
\emph{slower} at 6\arcmin\ (interval $[1.00, 1.08]$) and 8--15\% faster at 30\arcmin\
and 1\degr. This is a smaller and more ragged advantage than a phase-ordered measurement
suggests (Sect.~\ref{sec:phase}), and it is the number we stand behind.

Cold, the picture is different and more favourable: skycell is faster at every radius on
both corpora, by 5\% at 1\arcmin\ and by a factor 2--2.5 at 30\arcmin\ and beyond. The
reason is visible in the buffer counts of Table~\ref{tab:explain}: at 1\degr\ skycell
touches 55 pages against pgSphere's 154. When those pages are in memory the difference
is a few microseconds of memory traffic; when they must be read it is the whole cost of
the query. An index that wastes less area is worth most exactly when the data does not
fit in cache, which is the regime a large archive operates in.

Against Q3C, \texttt{skycell} is faster by a factor 4--50 depending on radius and cache
state. The gap at small radii has a specific and reportable cause: Q3C 2.0.5 expands
every \texttt{q3c\_radial\_query} into 100 key ranges regardless of radius, which costs
about 300 buffer pages and 1.2\,ms of planning before any row is read
(Table~\ref{tab:explain}). This is a property of that version's query path rather than of
the cube quad-tree; Q3C's cross-match path, which uses four ranges per probe, does not
pay it, and Sect.~\ref{sec:otherops} shows it performing accordingly.

\label{sec:scope}
We therefore state that result at three different strengths, because they are not
equally supported. Against \texttt{q3c\_radial\_query} as implemented in 2.0.5, the
factor 4--50 is a measurement we stand behind, and it is a comparison of
\emph{implementations}. Against \texttt{q3c\_join}, which the Q3C authors intend for
cross-matching and which does not carry the fixed expansion, the two are level
(Sect.~\ref{sec:xmatch}); that is the comparison we regard as informative about the
two indexing approaches. About the cube quad-tree as a \emph{method} we make no claim
at all: a covering-aware radial query in Q3C would plausibly close most of the cone
gap, and nothing here argues otherwise. pgSphere was used with the \texttt{spoint} and
\texttt{spoly} types and a GiST index as its documentation prescribes, at default
settings; we did not attempt to tune it further, and no method was given prepared
statements or plan reuse -- which would help \texttt{skycell} most, since planning is
the component that most differs between them.

The order effect the randomization exists to control is small but real: averaged over
all radii on the designed corpus, going first rather than last costs pgSphere 19\% and
skycell $-5$\% warm, while Q3C is flat. Since methods are assigned to positions at
random, this inflates the variance of the ratios rather than biasing them.

\subsection{Does the advantage grow with the catalogue?}
\label{sec:scale}

Ten million rows is 0.55\% of Gaia DR3, and the argument that an index matters most when
it does not fit in memory is an argument about scale. We therefore repeated the whole
protocol on a 50-million-row Gaia-density corpus, built identically. The point of that
size is specific: pgSphere's GiST index is then 3442\,MB against a 2\,GB
\texttt{shared\_buffers}, so it no longer fits, while \texttt{skycell}'s B-tree is
1072\,MB and still does.

The answer (Table~\ref{tab:scale}) divides by region size. For 30\arcmin\ and larger the
advantage grows markedly with the catalogue: at 1\degr\ the warm ratio improves from 0.85
to 0.60 and the cold ratio from 0.53 to 0.31, and the buffer counts show why -- 152 pages
against pgSphere's 514, where at $10^7$ rows it was 55 against 154. For cones of an
arcminute and below nothing improves: 1\arcsec\ stays at 0.89, and 1\arcmin\ moves from
0.93 to 1.02, no longer distinguishable from pgSphere. The reason is in the same table.
At those radii both methods touch five to seven pages whatever the table size, so there
is no scan left to shorten, and what remains is \texttt{skycell}'s planning -- 0.016 to
0.020\,ms against pgSphere's flat 0.009 -- which grows with source density because a
denser sky makes Eq.~(\ref{eq:sstar}) cut finer.

The rule that emerges is about pages rather than rows: \texttt{skycell} wins wherever a
query has enough pages to save, and the larger the catalogue the more that is worth. An
arcsecond cone on any catalogue is not the workload this index improves; a degree-scale
selection, a polygon or a cross-match on a large one is.

\subsection{How much the protocol matters}
\label{sec:phase}

Measuring one method at a time instead -- each method's whole grid, then the next --
gives \texttt{skycell}/pgSphere ratios of 0.69 to 0.87 below 30\arcmin\ where the
controlled protocol finds 0.89 to 1.03. The phase-ordered arrangement flatters whichever
method is measured later, and that was \texttt{skycell}. We note it because the
difference is larger than most of the effects this paper measures, and it is the reason
every comparison here is paired inside a trial.

dwarf them. A partitioned deployment changes the arithmetic, since only the partitions
in play need be resident. And the two B-trees are the same size, so the comparison is
really one geometry index plus one integer index against one integer index -- the saving
comes from not needing the geometry index at all, which rests on \texttt{skycell}
answering the region queries pgSphere is kept for, as Sects.~\ref{sec:cones}
and~\ref{sec:otherops} measure.

\subsection{Polygons and stored footprints}
\label{sec:otherops}

Convex polygons of 0.05\degr--2\degr\ take a median 0.281\,ms on the Gaia corpus
against pgSphere's 0.767\,ms and Q3C's 1.864\,ms, and 0.142\,ms against 0.631 and
1.526 on the designed corpus: a factor 2.7--4.4 either way; pgSphere's mean (4.2\,ms) and 95th
percentile (21\,ms) are far above its median, which the per-query records trace to
polygons whose bounding volume is a poor fit.

\subsection{Cross-matching}
\label{sec:xmatch}

Cross-matching a user's uploaded target list against the catalogue is the operation the
Gaia archive lists among its server-side capabilities \citep{gaiaarchive1}, and it is
Q3C's own speciality: \texttt{q3c\_join} is what most PostgreSQL archives use for it.
It therefore deserves the same protocol as the cone searches rather than a single
end-to-end run, and getting that protocol right turned out to matter more here than
anywhere else in this paper.

The probe table is split into eight blocks of 25\,000 probes, and a block is the unit
of pairing: within a block all four methods run back to back in an order drawn for that
block. Cache residency has to be controlled explicitly. At 50 million rows the three
catalogues and their indexes are about 14\,GB against 2\,GB of \texttt{shared\_buffers},
so a block's first touch is I/O bound -- the same \texttt{skycell} block query takes
6.2\,s cold and 0.32\,s once its pages are resident. Whichever method runs first on a
block therefore pays for the others, and that effect is larger than the difference
between the methods: in a first version of this measurement, running first cost
\texttt{skycell} a factor ten while leaving Q3C and pgSphere, whose own cost is several
seconds, visibly unchanged. Each method is consequently warmed on its own query for its
own block immediately before being timed, so that all four are measured in the same
state and what is compared is the work each does.

Table~\ref{tab:xmatch} gives the result at 50 million rows. All four methods return
identical row counts in every block.

\texttt{q3c\_join} is the fastest of the two established methods by a factor 2.4, which
is what its reputation predicts. \texttt{skycell}'s \texttt{LATERAL} form is level with
it -- 0.78 of its time at 1\arcsec\ with an interval that reaches 1.00, and 0.92 at
10\arcsec, not distinguishable -- and 2.5 to 3.3 times faster than pgSphere. The join
form, which emits a fixed number of range slots per outer row in the manner of
\texttt{q3c\_join}, is level with Q3C at 1\arcsec\ and 35\% slower at 10\arcsec, where a
fixed slot count fits the covering less well.

Why level rather than better is answerable from the covering itself. The sky a
covering scans, as a multiple of the sky asked for, is not monotonic in the radius.
Measured on the ObsCore relation of Sect.~\ref{sec:pressure} at the density of the
galactic centre, it is 21 at 0.1\arcsec, rises to about 105 between 0.25\arcsec\ and
1\arcsec, and then falls steadily: 34 at 10\arcsec, 11 at 50\arcsec, 4.6 at 6\arcmin,
1.34 at 1\degr\ and 1.07 above 30\degr. The worst point is sub-arcsecond, where the
cost model will not cut finer because the extra ranges would cost more than the area
they save, and so emits one or two very coarse cells. A cross-match at 1\arcsec\
therefore does some two orders of magnitude more area work than the region requires and
still draws level with \texttt{q3c\_join}; the same index at degree scale, where the
covering is within a third of the region, is where it wins outright. Tightening that
sub-arcsecond covering is an improvement independent of the planning cost, and neither
subsumes the other.

Which of \texttt{skycell}'s two forms is faster depends on the radius, and we were
wrong to report one of them as the faster. Repeating the measurement on the real
corpus across outer size, radius and target distribution (randomized method order
within a cell, each warmed on its own query, three repetitions), the \texttt{LATERAL}
form leads below about an arcsecond and loses badly above it: at $30\arcsec$ on
clustered targets it takes 5682\,ms against the fixed-slot form's 896\,ms. The
fixed-slot form is nearly flat in radius. Against \texttt{q3c\_join} the ratio over the
twelve cells runs from 0.60 to 1.73 with no consistent winner, and against pgSphere
\texttt{skycell} is 2--6 times faster throughout. The formulation is therefore a
variable of its own, not a detail of the index, and a single cross-match number
should not be read as a property of either method.

The claim this supports is the narrow one: on the operation Q3C is built for,
\texttt{skycell} is Q3C's equal rather than its better, while being two and a half to
three times faster than pgSphere -- and it reaches that position with the same index
that answers the region queries of Sect.~\ref{sec:cones}, which is the point of the
design.

\subsection{Estimates, plans and joins}
\label{sec:joins}

A spatial predicate is rarely alone. We ran three shapes -- a cone with a catalogue cut,
the same joined to an observation table, and a three-way join -- through each method,
recording the estimate, the plan strategy and the time separately, because they are
different claims.

The estimate advantage carries over: for queries returning rows, the median estimate
error at 30\arcmin--1\degr\ is 1.15--1.88 for skycell against 1.63--2.33 for Q3C and
2.50--3.00 for pgSphere.

The plans, however, are the same. Across 135 queries at 1\arcmin, 30\arcmin\ and
1\degr, all three methods chose the same strategy (nested loop over index scans) for
every query; skycell's better estimate did not change what the planner decided. Only at
3\degr, where a cone returns $\sim$15\,000 rows, did one query out of sixteen cross a
threshold: skycell's estimate led to a hash join where pgSphere's led to a nested loop,
and that query ran 1.29 times faster. Execution times in the join shapes are otherwise
within noise of pgSphere at 1\arcmin--1\degr.

The honest summary is that better estimates are demonstrated, better plans are not, and
better join times are not. The value of an accurate estimate is insurance against the
plan the optimiser would otherwise choose in a query we did not write; we have not shown
that insurance paying off on a workload we did write.

\subsection{The cost model's one parameter, and how to stop setting it}
\label{sec:calib}

Equation~(\ref{eq:cost}) has one free parameter, $c_{\rm range}$, and it need not be a
parameter at all: it is a ratio of two costs PostgreSQL already models. One more index
range is a B-tree descent, which \texttt{btcostestimate} charges as
$\lceil\log_2 N\rceil\cdot 50\,c_{\rm op}$ for the comparisons plus
$(H+1)\cdot 50\,c_{\rm op}$ for the pages; one false-positive row costs its index entry,
its heap tuple, the exact predicate, and the heap page amortised over the rows that
share it:

\begin{equation}
  c_{\rm range} = \frac{(\lceil\log_2 N\rceil + H + 1)\,50\,c_{\rm op}}
    {c_{\rm tuple} + c_{\rm itup} + k\,c_{\rm op} + c_{\rm page}/n_{\rm pp}},
  \label{eq:crange}
\end{equation}

with $n_{\rm pp} = \mathrm{reltuples}/\mathrm{relpages}$ the rows per heap page. Every
input is already at hand -- $\mathrm{relpages}$ sits beside $\mathrm{reltuples}$ in the
\texttt{pg\_class} tuple the density model loads, and the cost factors are the planner's
own GUCs -- so \texttt{skycell.range\_cost} defaults to evaluating it.

The last term is what makes this per-relation rather than per-installation. On the
catalogues, at 120 rows per page, Eq.~(\ref{eq:crange}) gives 116; on an ObsCore-shaped
relation, whose wider rows pack 54 to a page so that each false positive costs more page
traffic, it gives 93, asking for a finer covering; with the stock
\texttt{random\_page\_cost} of 4.0 instead of this host's 1.1 it gives 67.

What the derivation buys is not speed. Placed in the same randomized trials as the fixed
constant it replaces, the two are indistinguishable at every radius on the
50-million-row corpus (ratios 0.93 to 1.08, every interval containing 1), because the
cost curve is flat: sweeping $c_{\rm range}$ over four decades moves the median time by
less than 25\% anywhere between 3 and 300, and at 1\degr\ every setting from 3 to 300
lies within 3\%. What it buys is that the parameter stops being a number someone has to
choose, adapts to row width and to the storage the operator has described to the
planner, and cannot be left at a value tuned for a different relation.

The order the model picks is likewise close to optimal: measured with two orders
interleaved per query, its choice costs about 3\% against one order finer at 6\arcmin.
The flat curve is again the reason, and it is why the cost model does not need to be
right -- it needs to land in the flat region, and it does.

\subsection{The density estimate against the truth}
\label{sec:estimator}

The cost model's input is $\hat\rho$ from Eq.~(\ref{eq:rho}). An earlier version of this
work called it unbiased. That was a statement about the corpora we had: positions
resampled from an order-9 density map contain no structure below $0.11\degr$, so the
histogram cannot fail to resolve what is not there. On real Gaia DR3 positions it fails,
and where it fails is not random.

Table~\ref{tab:estimator} compares $\hat\rho$ with $\rho$ counted from the same
relation, at six positions spanning four decades of surface density. On ordinary sky the
estimate is good to about $\pm25\%$. In the core of a globular cluster it is \emph{low
by a factor 8--12}, and at the Galactic centre \emph{high by a factor 3.7}. The complete,
full-density fields show the mechanism directly: at radii $\le 0.05\degr$ the estimate is
excellent (0.97--1.05 at $\omega$~Cen, the LMC and Baade's Window) and collapses to
0.15--0.23 at $0.2\degr$. The histogram resolves a cluster while the query sits inside it
and averages it away once the query is larger than it. This is the failure an equi-depth
histogram over a space-filling key is expected to have (Sect.~\ref{sec:density}); what is
new here is that it is measured, on real positions, rather than argued about.

The consequence for the method is smaller than the size of the error suggests, and
Sect.~\ref{sec:ablation} measures it: because $s^{*} \propto \sqrt{\rho}$, an order of
magnitude in $\rho$ is a factor three in cell size, about one and a half orders, and the
cost curve is flat across that. The covering the model chooses in a cluster core, where
$\hat\rho$ is wrong by an order of magnitude, is within 1.09--1.43 of the best fixed
order -- no worse than where the estimate is accurate. The quantity to judge the
estimator by is the decision it produces, not the number it reports.

\subsection{What the advantage is made of}
\label{sec:ablation}

The comparison with pgSphere confounds three things: a covering whose resolution
adapts, a compact B-tree over an integer key rather than a GiST over bounding boxes,
and a heap laid out in the same space-filling order as the index. On the real Gaia
corpus they separate as follows.

\emph{Heap clustering} is a large absolute effect and almost no differential one. The
same ten million rows, built once in cell order and once in \texttt{random\_index}
order -- random on the sky -- cost 1.38, 1.66 and 2.08 times more unclustered for
\texttt{skycell} at $10\arcsec$, $0.1\degr$ and $1\degr$, and 1.55, 1.69 and 2.22 times
more for pgSphere. The ratio between the two methods barely moves (0.64 clustered
against 0.60 unclustered at $1\degr$). Physically ordered tables therefore do not
account for the comparison; they make both methods faster by about the same factor.

\emph{The adaptive resolution} is worth a great deal. Measured against every fixed
order from 4 to 13, the penalty for choosing a fixed order badly is 345, 243 and 830
times at the three radii, and the best fixed order moves from 12--13 at $10\arcsec$ to
7--8 at $0.5\degr$: no single resolution is defensible across the range. The cost
model, which does not know the answer, lands within 1.02--1.58 of the best (median
1.17) -- including in cluster cores where its density input is wrong by an order of
magnitude, which is the evidence behind Sect.~\ref{sec:estimator}.

\emph{The index representation} is isolated by comparing against Q3C rather than
pgSphere, since both are B-trees over an \texttt{int8} key: what differs is the
covering strategy alone. We report that comparison in Sect.~\ref{sec:cones}, with the
caveat of Sect.~\ref{sec:scope} that it is dominated by one version's fixed expansion
into a hundred ranges.

\section{Inside a TAP service, and where the crossover is}
\label{sec:tap}

\subsection{The service-level A/B}

A benchmark of index operations is not a benchmark of an archive. We ran the same build
inside \texttt{egernia} \citep{egernia}, a TAP 1.1 server, against its published
500\,096-row ObsCore corpus: one database holding both indexes, two otherwise identical
service processes differing only in how they translate \texttt{CONTAINS}, and the
service's own harness interleaving them rung by rung over six query classes at 1, 8 and 32
concurrent clients, three repetitions each.

Both targets pass the conformance gate (\texttt{taplint}, zero errors) and the
cross-server agreement gate on all eleven portable query classes, and the 203 cone
queries of the corpus return identical rows through both translations. The performance
result is a tie: all 18 comparison cells fall inside the tie rule, with throughput ratios
between 0.96 and 1.06, although at the database level \texttt{skycell} is 1.2--1.45 times
\emph{slower} per query on that corpus.

Both facts have the same cause. A cone over half a million rows returns tens to hundreds
of rows and costs a fraction of a millisecond either way, against roughly 10\,ms of CPU
per request spent in the service's own Python; the database is 1--3\% of a request, so a
difference of 0.08\,ms in it cannot move the throughput of the service.

\subsection{Where the crossover is}

That explanation makes a testable prediction, and an earlier version of this work
asserted it rather than testing it. We built the same ObsCore-shaped relation --
observations in groups of 128 products sharing a field centre, clustered by collection
and field, which is what makes a GiST bitmap scan so effective on this data -- at three
sizes, and ran the same four cone classes against both indexes
(Table~\ref{tab:crossover}).

The crossover is real, but it is not a single number. For the cone with catalogue cuts
(Q07) and the degree-scale cone (Q06), \texttt{skycell} is ahead at every size and pulls
further ahead as the relation grows, reading a seventh of pgSphere's buffer pages at Q06
and a quarter at Q07; the empty cone (Q12) is level. The small-cone class (Q05,
0.15\degr) we cannot pin down: paired inside a trial it is 2.16 times slower immediately
after the build, and level (0.94) on the same relation once thoroughly warm. Those are
different cache regimes, and on this host we could not make that one class reproducible to
better than a factor of two. The deficit is at least localised -- it is in execution
(0.324\,ms against 0.161), not planning (0.034 against 0.009), and not the exact
predicate, whose removal changes the time by about 1\% here against 63\% on the
catalogue.

\subsection{When the index no longer fits in memory}
\label{sec:pressure}

Section~\ref{sec:scale-memory} argues from index size alone that a host must reach a
point at which pgSphere's index no longer stays in memory while \texttt{skycell}'s still
does. That is an argument about arithmetic, so we tested it. A container limited to
3\,GiB, with 768\,MB of shared buffers, holds an ObsCore-shaped relation of
20\,485\,632 rows whose heap is 22\,GB. The GiST index over \texttt{spoint} is 1162\,MB
(59.5\,B/row, $1.51\times$ the buffer pool); the \texttt{skycell} expression index is
439\,MB (22.5\,B/row, $0.57\times$).

Making the cache matter at all required a query stream that sweeps the sky. Twenty
thousand cones of 0.15\degr\ cover 3.4\% of it and read essentially nothing from disk at
either index size: what has to fit is not the index, but the part of it the stream
touches. We therefore used 3500 cones of 2\degr\ drawn uniformly, which cover the sphere
about once.

Each index was measured in its own phase, with the server restarted between phases so
that the buffer pool starts empty and the index not under test occupies none of it --
checked after every phase, where it holds 0 bytes. Each phase warms itself with an
untimed sweep and then measures a second sweep in a fixed random order; both orderings of
the two phases were run (Table~\ref{tab:pressure}). \texttt{skycell} answers the stream
4.2--4.8 times faster, and the mechanism is visible in the index traffic: it serves 53\%
of its index accesses from the pool against pgSphere's 9\%. The buffer counts reproduce
to within one page across the two orderings, so the spread in the medians is host drift
rather than an ordering effect.

Two qualifications matter. First, this is not the clean threshold the size arithmetic
suggests. The 22\,GB heap takes 705--734\,MB of the 768\,MB pool in every phase, so
\emph{neither} index is resident; what is being measured is a smaller index winning a
contested pool, not one index fitting while the other does not. Second, the effect is
easy to measure away. Running both translations in a single pass over the same centres --
the obvious design, and the one we tried first -- puts the gap at $1.6\times$ rather than
$4.2$, because the two answer the same cone from the same heap pages and each leaves them
warm for the other.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

These measurements support a bounded claim. A covering sized by cost over an equal-area
integer key is a sound basis for a sky index: on 10- and 50-million-row catalogues it is
faster than pgSphere for cone searches at most radii warm and at every radius cold, by
the largest margins where an index should matter most -- large regions, cold caches,
polygons -- and it draws level with Q3C on cross-matching, the operation Q3C exists for.
It does this with an index a third the size, built thirty times faster, and with row
estimates twice as accurate. The same conclusions hold on a density field taken from the
real sky and on one designed by us, and the advantage on large regions roughly doubles
between $10^7$ and $5\times10^7$ rows, which is the evidence that the page count, and
not something particular to one table size, produces it.

They do not establish that \texttt{skycell} is the better choice in general. Warm,
between 6\arcmin\ and 1\degr, it is indistinguishable from pgSphere on one corpus and
slightly slower at 6\arcmin\ on the other. On a 500\,000-row ObsCore relation it is
slower; on the small-cone ObsCore class it is slower in one cache regime and level in
another, and we do not claim that row. Better row estimates did not, on the join shapes
we wrote, produce better plans. And nothing here has run on real catalogue positions, on
more than one machine, or with more than one storage device.

What remains expensive is planning. A covering is computed per plan, and even cached it
costs 0.015--0.12\,ms against 0.006--0.008\,ms for a GiST predicate that needs no such
step; that is the whole of \texttt{skycell}'s disadvantage at 6\arcmin\ and the reason
small relations favour pgSphere. The architectural way out is to stop planning ranges: an
SP-GiST operator class over the cell hierarchy would present a single index
qualification and refine during the index descent, where the work is proportional to the
rows actually present, removing both the per-plan covering and the per-arm planner cost
our measurements put at 8\,\textmu s for each range in the disjunction.

The density synopsis is the other limit. An equi-depth histogram cannot see a cluster
smaller than one of its buckets, and because it is equi-depth, raising the statistics
target does not help -- a denser region gets narrower buckets, so interpolating inside
one is scale invariant. The area guard bounds the damage; a custom \texttt{typanalyze}
storing a multi-order count map would estimate what the covering actually needs. We have
implemented such a map as an option and have no measurement showing it helps, which is
why it is not part of the result above.

\section{Conclusions}

\texttt{skycell} keeps Q3C's compact integer key and B-tree while answering the region
queries pgSphere was built for, by treating the depth of a covering as a cost question
whose answer follows from the local source density and the price of an index range.

Under a protocol designed to remove the advantages that measurement order and cache
state hand to whichever method is measured last, on two 10-million-row corpora -- one
drawn from the real Gaia DR3 density field -- it is 6--11\% faster than pgSphere for
cone radii of 1\arcsec--1\arcmin\ and 31--37\% faster at 3\degr\ warm, and faster at
every radius with caches dropped, by a factor of up to 2.5. It answers convex polygons
2.7--4.4 times faster, keeps an index a third the size of pgSphere's -- 38\,GiB against
160\,GiB at Gaia DR3 scale for the pair of indexes that covers the same ground, which is
the difference between a 64\,GiB server and a 256\,GiB one -- builds it thirty
times faster, and estimates row counts twice as accurately. On cross-matching, the
operation Q3C exists for, it is Q3C's equal (0.78--0.92 of its time, intervals reaching
1) and 2.5--3.3 times faster than pgSphere -- so one index is competitive with each of
the two established ones on the workload that each was built for.
Its cost model's one parameter is derived from the relation's rows per page and the
planner's cost factors rather than configured -- indistinguishable in speed from the
constant it replaces, but no longer a number anyone has to choose -- its chosen cell
order costs about 3\% against the best available one, measured with the two interleaved,
and its density estimate, though poor in cluster cores, is accurate enough to pick a
covering within 1.6 of the best available one.

It is not the better choice everywhere, and scale sharpens the distinction rather than
removing it. Raising the catalogue to 50 million rows widens the advantage for regions of
30\arcmin\ and above -- to a factor 3.2 at 1\degr\ with cold caches -- and leaves cones of
an arcminute and below where they were, level with pgSphere, because there both methods
touch a handful of pages at any table size and what remains is planning. On a small
relation \texttt{skycell} is slower for that reason, and inside a TAP server whose cost is
dominated by Python it changes nothing at all. On an ObsCore-shaped relation it leads the
degree-scale classes at every size measured while the arcminute-scale class depends on the
cache regime by a factor of two in either direction, which we do not claim.

An index is worth choosing carefully when the scan dominates. Which queries those are is
a property of the archive, and we have tried to say precisely which ones they were here.
